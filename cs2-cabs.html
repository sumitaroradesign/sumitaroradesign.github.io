01

What this product is, and why it matters

This is an enterprise platform used across IKEA to plan and brief large-scale communication projects — catalogues, brochures, and web-based campaigns. It sits at the centre of a complex planning process that spans content selection, market alignment, supply chain coordination, and execution briefing.

Planning communication at this scale requires answering complex questions simultaneously: which products are available across which markets, for how long, during which campaign windows, and at what level of supply chain readiness? Before this platform existed, every one of those questions was answered manually.

Who uses it

  • Communication planners across business areas
  • Market alignment teams
  • Supply chain and availability teams
  • Content and creative teams downstream

What it supports

  • Campaign content planning and structure
  • Product selection based on business and market rules
  • Market-specific layout and content variation
  • Structured briefs for downstream execution
My role

Understanding a process, then redesigning it

I led the UX and product strategy for this platform — from early research into how communication planning actually happened, through workflow redesign, system design, and iterative delivery with stakeholders across business areas, product, and technology.

The starting point wasn't a brief for a new tool. It was a recognition that the existing process — heavily manual, disconnected, and siloed by business area — was no longer sustainable at the scale IKEA needed to operate.

02

A process built on manual effort and individual expertise

Each home furnishing business area worked in its own silo — in spreadsheets and PowerPoints — with no collaborative way to plan, no shared data, and no system to enforce business rules or validate decisions.

Problem 01
No way to know which products were right
Users had to run multiple reports, pull them into a common file, and manually work out which products were available in which markets, across which campaign windows. There was no system to surface this — only effort.
Problem 02
Business areas working in silos
Each team worked independently in their own files. There was no shared workspace, no visibility into what other areas were doing, and coordination happened through meetings and email — not through the system.
Problem 03
Supply alignment took weeks, not days
Preparing product lists for classification and supply alignment — ensuring high availability across all systems — took up to 15 days for around 30 users. This was a structural bottleneck, not an edge case.
Problem 04
Validating decisions, not making them
The majority of planning time was spent checking whether a decision was valid — not on the decision itself. Users were human validation engines, running business rules that a system should have been enforcing.
Problem 05
No brief creation process
Once products were selected and pages planned, there was no structured way to communicate decisions downstream. Briefs were assembled manually and lacked the consistency the value chain needed to act on them.
Problem 06
Market variation handled ad hoc
Different markets sell different products. Preparing additional pages for markets not selling a featured product, and managing those layout differences, was entirely manual — and easily missed.
03

Research & insights

To understand the problem deeply, I focused on how communication planning was actually carried out at the business area level — not the ideal process, but the real one. I worked closely with stakeholders to map the end-to-end workflow, identifying key milestones and measuring how long different tasks actually took.

"Users were spending more time validating decisions than making them. The problem wasn't capability — it was the absence of a system that could support decision-making."

Step 01 — Workflow mapping
Understanding planning at the business area level
I worked directly with planners to trace the end-to-end workflow — from first product consideration through to brief submission. This revealed where time was being spent and where decisions were being made versus validated.
Step 02 — Time analysis
Quantifying the manual effort
By measuring how long key tasks took — particularly product validation and supply list preparation — it became clear that the volume of manual work was not just inefficient, it was structurally incompatible with the pace of campaign planning.
Step 03 — Pain synthesis
Identifying the disconnects
I identified the major points where system support was missing — where adding a feature or enforcing a business rule in the system would eliminate a manual step entirely. These became the core design opportunities.
Step 04 — Stakeholder alignment
Building shared understanding across teams
Because planning touched so many teams — business areas, supply chain, technology, product — establishing a shared understanding of the problem was as important as the design work itself. Research became a communication tool.
04

Insight to solution

The solutions were shaped directly by the research — each addressing a specific disconnect between what the process required and what the system provided.

Insight
Running reports manually to find the right products was unsustainable
Users spent the majority of planning time verifying eligibility — not choosing products. The system knew the rules; it just wasn't applying them.
Solution — System-driven product validation
  • Business rules encoded into the platform, not held in people's heads
  • Products surfaced based on availability, market fit, and campaign timing
  • Eliminated the need to run and reconcile multiple reports
Insight
Supply alignment was taking 15 days because it was entirely manual
Preparing product lists for classification and supply readiness was a 15-day process for ~30 users. It was structured work being done in an unstructured way.
Solution — Streamlined supply preparation workflow
  • Systematized the list preparation steps into a guided workflow
  • Reduced 15-day process to approximately 3 days
  • Consistent output format, ready for downstream systems
Insight
Teams were collaborating around the system, not inside it
Without a shared workspace, each business area worked in isolation. Alignment happened through meetings and file-sharing — slow, fragile, and easy to lose.
Solution — Shared planning workspace
  • Single environment for all business areas to plan together
  • Live visibility into what other teams are working on
  • Replaced parallel spreadsheets with a common source of truth
Insight
Market differences were handled reactively, not by design
Not every market sells the same products. Managing layout variations for markets not featuring a product was a manual afterthought — and often missed.
Solution — Market-aware layout planning
  • System flags market-specific variations at the planning stage
  • Alternative layouts defined within the same workflow
  • Reduced errors and last-minute corrections downstream
Insight
Briefs were assembled manually from planning output
Once decisions were made, communicating them downstream required another round of manual work — assembling briefs that the value chain then had to interpret and act on.
Solution — Structured brief generation
  • Briefs generated directly from planning decisions
  • Consistent, structured format for downstream execution
  • Eliminated the gap between planning and communication

06

What I took away

Learning 01
Business rules belong in the system, not in people
When users spend their time enforcing rules that could be automated, the design problem isn't usability — it's misplaced responsibility. Encoding logic into the system freed people to focus on actual decisions.
Learning 02
Collaboration needs a place to happen
Teams weren't failing to collaborate because they didn't want to. They were collaborating in email and meetings because the system gave them no alternative. A shared workspace changed the nature of how teams worked together.
Learning 03
Small time savings at scale are large
Saving 30 minutes per user per planning cycle sounds modest. Across 30 users, multiple cycles per year, and years of operation, it compounds into something material. Research into time-on-task is worth the investment.
Learning 04
The planner needs guidance, not just data
Users didn't lack access to information — they lacked the system support to act on it confidently. The design shift from surfacing raw data to guiding decisions was the most impactful single change in the platform.
